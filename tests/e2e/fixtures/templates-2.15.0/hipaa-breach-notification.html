3. Definitions

  • Breach (164.402): the acquisition, access, use or disclosure of PHI in a manner not permitted by the Privacy Rule that compromises its security or privacy. An impermissible use or disclosure is presumed to be a breach unless a documented risk assessment (section 6) shows a low probability that the PHI has been compromised.
  • Exceptions: (1) unintentional, good-faith acquisition, access or use by a workforce member or person acting under the authority of {{client_name}} or a business associate, within the scope of authority and not further improperly used or disclosed; (2) inadvertent disclosure between persons authorized to access PHI at the same covered entity, business associate or organized health care arrangement, not further improperly used or disclosed; (3) disclosure where there is a good-faith belief the unauthorized recipient could not reasonably have retained the information.
  • Unsecured PHI: PHI not rendered unusable, unreadable or indecipherable to unauthorized persons through a technology or methodology specified in HHS guidance — in practice, encryption consistent with NIST guidance (e.g. NIST SP 800-111 for data at rest; NIST SP 800-52, 800-77 or 800-113 for data in transit) with the key not compromised, or destruction consistent with NIST SP 800-88. Breaches of properly encrypted or destroyed PHI do not require notification under HIPAA.
  • Discovery (164.404(a)(2)): a breach is treated as discovered on the first day it is known to {{client_name}} — or, by exercising reasonable diligence, would have been known — to any workforce member or agent other than the person who committed it. Notification clocks start at discovery, not at the end of the investigation.

4. Reporting a suspected breach

Workforce members must immediately report any suspected impermissible use or disclosure of PHI — misdirected email or fax, lost or stolen device, unauthorized record access, ransomware, phishing compromise, improper disposal — to the Privacy Official and {{company_name}}. Do not investigate on your own, delete evidence, or contact affected patients until instructed.

5. Initial response

  1. Record the date and time of discovery and who reported it.
  2. Contain the incident (retrieve misdirected information, disable accounts, isolate systems, remote-wipe devices) with {{company_name}}.
  3. Notify legal counsel and the cyber insurance carrier as the policy requires.
  4. Preserve evidence: logs, emails, device images, access reports.
  5. Determine what PHI was involved, how many individuals, their states of residence, and whether the PHI was secured (encrypted).

6. Four-factor risk assessment (164.402(2))

Unless an exception applies or the PHI was secured, assess at least these factors and document the conclusion:

  1. Nature and extent of the PHI involved, including the types of identifiers and the likelihood of re-identification (e.g. Social Security numbers, financial data, diagnoses, and sensitive categories such as mental health or substance use information weigh toward higher risk).
  2. The unauthorized person who used the PHI or to whom it was disclosed (e.g. another HIPAA covered entity is lower risk than an unknown attacker).
  3. Whether the PHI was actually acquired or viewed, or only the opportunity existed (e.g. forensic analysis shows a stolen laptop was never accessed).
  4. The extent to which the risk has been mitigated (e.g. signed attestation from the recipient that the information was destroyed and not further disclosed).

7. Notice to individuals (164.404)

  • Timing: without unreasonable delay and in no case later than 60 calendar days after discovery. The 60 days is an outer limit; do not wait if the facts are known sooner. (California laws in section 12 may require faster notice.)
  • Method: written notice by first-class mail to the last known address, or by email if the individual agreed to electronic notice. For a deceased individual, notify the next of kin or personal representative if the address is known. If there is possible imminent misuse, also contact individuals by telephone.
  • Substitute notice when contact information is insufficient or out of date: fewer than 10 people — alternative written notice, telephone or other means; 10 or more — a conspicuous posting on the website home page for 90 days or notice in major print or broadcast media where affected individuals likely live, plus a toll-free number active for at least 90 days.
  • Required content (plain language):
    1. A brief description of what happened, including the date of the breach and the date of discovery, if known.
    2. The types of unsecured PHI involved (e.g. name, Social Security number, date of birth, home address, account number, diagnosis, disability code).
    3. Steps individuals should take to protect themselves from potential harm.
    4. A brief description of what {{client_name}} is doing to investigate, mitigate harm and protect against further breaches.
    5. Contact procedures for questions, which must include a toll-free telephone number, an email address, website or postal address.

8. Notice to HHS (164.408)

  • 500 or more individuals: notify the Secretary of HHS through the OCR breach portal (ocrportal.hhs.gov) contemporaneously with individual notice and within 60 calendar days of discovery.
  • Fewer than 500 individuals: log the breach and submit it through the OCR portal no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar year in which the breach was discovered (i.e. by about March 1). Individual notice is still due within 60 days of discovery.
  • Breach log for this year: [location of log].

9. Notice to the media (164.406)

If a breach involves more than 500 residents of a state or jurisdiction, notify prominent media outlets serving that state or jurisdiction without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery, with the same content as the individual notice. Coordinate the press release with counsel and the insurance carrier.

10. Business associates (164.410)

  • A business associate (including {{company_name}} where it has access to PHI) must notify {{client_name}} of a breach of unsecured PHI without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery, providing, to the extent possible, the identity of each affected individual and the information needed for the individual notice.
  • BAAs may set shorter reporting timeframes; check each BAA (see the BAA Checklist & Inventory).
  • If a breach occurs at a business associate, {{client_name}} (the covered entity) remains responsible for notifying individuals, HHS and the media unless that duty has been delegated in writing. The discovery date is generally the date the business associate discovered it if the business associate is acting as an agent.

11. Law enforcement delay (164.412)

If a law enforcement official states that notice would impede a criminal investigation or damage national security: if the statement is in writing and specifies a time period, delay notice for that period; if the statement is oral, document it (official's name, agency, date) and delay no longer than 30 days unless a written statement is provided during that time.

12. California requirements

California law may impose shorter deadlines or additional recipients. Confirm with counsel which apply:

  • Licensed clinics, health facilities, home health agencies and hospices (Health & Safety Code 1280.15): report unlawful or unauthorized access to, or use or disclosure of, a patient's medical information to the California Department of Public Health (CDPH) and to the affected patient or representative no later than 15 business days after it is detected, subject to law enforcement delay. CDPH regulations (22 CCR) set out reporting details and penalties. Applies to facilities licensed under H&S Code 1204, 1250, 1725 or 1745. [Applies to us: yes / no]
  • General breach notice (Civil Code 1798.82): businesses must notify California residents whose unencrypted personal information (which includes medical and health insurance information) was, or is reasonably believed to have been, acquired by an unauthorized person. As amended by SB 446 (effective January 1, 2026), notice is due within 30 calendar days of discovery, subject to the legitimate needs of law enforcement or as necessary to determine the scope of the breach and restore the integrity of the system. If more than 500 California residents are notified, a sample copy must be submitted to the California Attorney General (oag.ca.gov) within 15 calendar days of notifying consumers. The statute has specific format and heading requirements, and a provision addressing HIPAA covered entities that comply with HITECH notice content — verify how it applies before relying on the HIPAA 60-day window.
  • Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (Civil Code 56 et seq.): may create additional liability for unauthorized disclosure of medical information.
  • Other states: if affected individuals live in other states, those states' notification laws may apply.

13. Documentation and burden of proof (164.414, 164.530(j))

{{client_name}} has the burden of demonstrating that all required notifications were made or that an incident was not a breach. For every incident, keep: the incident report, investigation notes, the four-factor risk assessment, copies of notices and proof of mailing/posting, HHS submission confirmation, media notices, law enforcement delay statements, and corrective actions. Retain documentation for at least six years. Breach records are kept at [location].

14. Training and sanctions

Workforce members are trained on recognizing and reporting breaches at onboarding and annually. Workforce members who fail to report or who cause a breach through policy violations are subject to the sanctions policy (164.530(e)).
