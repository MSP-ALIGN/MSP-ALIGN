Password & Multi-Factor Authentication Policy

{{client_name}} · Effective {{today}} · Maintained with {{company_name}}

1. Purpose

Stolen and guessed passwords are the most common way attackers get into small businesses. This policy sets requirements for passwords, password managers and multi-factor authentication (MFA) at {{client_name}}. It follows current guidance in NIST SP 800-63B-4 (August 2025), which favors long passphrases, screening against breached passwords and strong MFA over complexity rules and frequent forced changes. Where a password is the only factor (an approved MFA exception), it must be at least 15 characters.

2. Scope

This policy applies to all workforce members, contractors and vendors, and to every account used to access {{client_name}} systems or data: Microsoft 365 / Entra ID or Google Workspace, computers, servers, line-of-business applications, banking, payroll and tax portals, vendor portals, network devices, and social media accounts managed on behalf of the company.

3. Roles and responsibilities

  • Security lead ({{field:security_lead}}): owns this policy and approves exceptions.
  • {{company_name}}: configures password and MFA settings in identity systems, deploys and administers the password manager, monitors risky sign-ins, and assists users with MFA enrollment and recovery.
  • Users: create strong passphrases, store them in the password manager, protect MFA devices, and report suspected compromise immediately.

4. Password requirements

  • Length: user passwords must be at least 14 characters. Passphrases of several unrelated words (for example granite-bicycle-orchard-lamp) are encouraged. Administrator passwords must be at least 16 characters, and service account passwords at least 25 random characters.
  • Complexity: systems will not require arbitrary character-type rules where they can be configured not to. All printable characters, including spaces, are allowed. Systems must accept passwords of at least 64 characters. Where cardholder data is in scope, passwords must also contain both letters and numbers (PCI DSS 8.3.6).
  • Breached and weak password screening: new passwords are checked against lists of known-breached, common and context-specific passwords (such as the company name, "Password", seasons plus years, or the user's name). {{company_name}} enables Entra ID Password Protection with a custom banned list, or an equivalent control, for on-premises Active Directory and cloud accounts.
  • Uniqueness: each account must have its own password. Company passwords must never be reused on personal or other accounts.
  • Password history: where the system supports it, a new password cannot match any of the last [4/12/24] passwords (at least 4 where cardholder data is in scope).
  • Temporary and initial passwords: passwords set by {{company_name}} for a new account or a reset are unique to the user, given to the user through a separate channel from the username, valid only for the first sign-in, and must be changed immediately.
  • No periodic forced rotation: passwords are not required to change on a schedule. A password must be changed immediately when there is any sign it may be compromised: it appears in a breach notification, it was entered on a phishing page, it was shared, or a device it was stored on is lost or infected. Where a regulation or contract still requires periodic rotation for single-factor accounts (for example PCI DSS 8.3.9), that requirement applies to those accounts only.
  • No hints or knowledge questions: password hints and "security questions" are not used where they can be disabled. Where a site requires them, store random answers in the password manager.
  • Default passwords: all vendor default passwords on devices and applications (routers, firewalls, printers, cameras, NAS devices) are changed before the device is put into use.
  • Storage and transmission: passwords are never written on paper near a device, stored in spreadsheets, text files, browser notes or email, or sent by email or text. Systems must store passwords only as salted hashes and send them only over encrypted connections. Passwords are never hard-coded in scripts, configuration files or source code; scripts use the password manager, a secrets vault or a managed identity instead.
  • Hidden entry: sign-in screens mask passwords as they are typed (for example with dots), and users do not enter passwords where others can watch.

5. Password manager

  • {{client_name}} provides an approved business password manager ({{field:password_manager}}) administered by {{company_name}}. All workforce members must use it for company credentials.
  • The master password must be a passphrase of at least 16 characters that is not used anywhere else, and the password manager account must be protected with MFA.
  • Shared credentials that cannot be avoided (for example a vendor portal that allows only one login) are stored in shared vaults with access limited to named users; access is removed at offboarding.
  • Browser-built-in password saving is disabled on company devices where practical, in favor of the approved password manager.
  • On departure, the user's company vault is transferred to the manager or security lead and the account is disabled.

6. Multi-factor authentication

  • MFA is required for: email and collaboration (Microsoft 365 / Google Workspace); all remote access (VPN, ZTNA, remote desktop gateways); all cloud applications that support it, including banking, payroll, accounting, tax software and line-of-business SaaS; the password manager; and all administrative and privileged access, including to servers, firewalls and cloud admin portals.
  • Where an application cannot do MFA itself, access is placed behind single sign-on (SSO) with MFA or behind a VPN/ZTNA that requires MFA. Systems that cannot meet this are recorded as exceptions.
  • Preferred methods, strongest first:
    1. Phishing-resistant methods: FIDO2 security keys, passkeys, Windows Hello for Business, or certificate-based authentication.
    2. Authenticator app push notifications with number matching and application context (such as Microsoft Authenticator).
    3. Authenticator app one-time codes (TOTP).
  • MFA applies to anyone, including employees, contractors, vendors and {{company_name}} staff, who accesses any system that holds customer information or Restricted data, and to all access into the cardholder data environment where one exists.
  • SMS text, voice call and email one-time codes are disabled in the identity system where a stronger option is available. They are permitted only for vendor systems that offer nothing else, and those systems are listed as exceptions.
  • Administrators (global admins, domain admins, firewall and backup admins, {{company_name}} technicians accessing client tenants, including for remote maintenance) must use phishing-resistant MFA.
  • Hardware security keys, smart cards and certificates are issued to one named person and are never shared. Lost keys are reported and revoked the same day.
  • Conditional access policies (or Security Defaults where conditional access is not licensed) require MFA for all users, block legacy authentication protocols that bypass MFA (such as basic authentication for POP, IMAP and SMTP), and block or force a secure password change for high-risk users and high-risk sign-ins where licensing allows.
  • Access to company data from computers is limited to devices that are managed by {{company_name}} and compliant (or hybrid-joined): {{field:mfa_scope}}.
  • MFA cannot be bypassed: there are no "remember this device forever" exceptions for administrators, and any trusted location exclusions are limited to [office IP addresses / none] and recorded.
  • Users must never approve an MFA prompt they did not initiate, and must never read an MFA code to anyone, including someone claiming to be from {{company_name}} or a bank. Unexpected prompts must be denied and reported immediately; repeated prompts ("MFA fatigue") are a sign the password has been stolen.
  • MFA re-registration, resetting a user's MFA, or resetting a password requires identity verification by {{company_name}}, such as a call-back to a known phone number or manager confirmation. Help desk resets are never done solely on the strength of a caller's claim.
  • Self-service password reset is [enabled / not enabled] for users and, when enabled, requires the user's registered MFA methods rather than security questions.

7. Account lockout and session controls

  • Accounts lock after no more than 10 consecutive failed sign-in attempts for at least 30 minutes, or until unlocked by {{company_name}} after verification. Cloud identity systems may use smart lockout that blocks attackers without locking out the real user.
  • Laptops, phones and tablets lock or wipe after repeated failed unlock attempts: no more than [10] attempts on phones and tablets and [20] on laptops.
  • Workstations lock after 15 minutes of inactivity and require re-authentication. Mobile devices lock after 5 minutes or less.
  • Sign-in monitoring: {{company_name}} monitors failed sign-ins, lockouts, impossible-travel and other risky sign-in alerts, and investigates repeated failures or unusual locations as described in the Logging & Monitoring Policy.
  • Remote and privileged sessions follow the session timeout rules in the Remote Access Policy.

8. Compromised credentials

If a user suspects a password or MFA method has been exposed, they must contact {{company_name}} at {{company_emergency_phone}} immediately. {{company_name}} will reset the password, revoke sessions and tokens, review sign-in logs and mailbox rules, and follow the Incident Response Plan if unauthorized access occurred.

9. Communicating this policy

This policy and the steps for setting up MFA, using the password manager and reporting a compromised password are given to every user at onboarding and covered in security awareness training. Users acknowledge that they have read it.

10. Exceptions

Systems that cannot meet these requirements (for example legacy software that limits password length or has no MFA) must be documented with compensating controls such as network restriction, SSO or VPN-only access, and approved by the security lead. Exceptions are reviewed at least annually.

11. Enforcement

Sharing passwords, disabling MFA, or approving unsolicited MFA prompts may result in disciplinary action up to and including termination. Technical controls enforce these requirements wherever possible.
