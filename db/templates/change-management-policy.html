Change Management Policy

{{client_name}} · Effective {{today}} · Maintained with {{company_name}}

1. Purpose

This policy ensures that changes to {{client_name}}'s IT systems are planned, reviewed, approved, tested and documented, so that changes don't cause unexpected outages, weaken security, or leave the business without a record of what was done. It is designed to be practical for a small business: routine work moves quickly, while risky changes get the right review.

2. Scope

This policy applies to changes to production systems, including servers and virtual machines, network equipment (firewalls, switches, wireless), cloud tenants and security settings (Microsoft 365 / Entra ID, conditional access, mail flow), line-of-business applications, backup systems, endpoint security and management policies, DNS and domains, and phone systems. It applies to changes made by {{company_name}}, software vendors and any {{client_name}} staff with administrative access.

Out of scope: individual user support tasks (password resets, adding a user to an existing role group, installing an approved application on one device), which are handled as normal service tickets.

3. Roles and responsibilities

  • Change requester: anyone asking for a change; describes what is needed and why.
  • Change implementer: usually a {{company_name}} technician or vendor; plans, tests, implements and documents the change.
  • Change approver(s): for normal changes, the {{company_name}} service lead or {{vcio_name}} plus the {{client_name}} business owner of the affected system ([name, title]). For high-risk changes, the security lead or executive sponsor also approves.
  • Security lead ([name, title]): owns this policy on the client side, approves high-risk and security-related changes, and reviews emergency changes after the fact.
  • {{company_name}}: maintains the change process in its ticketing system and keeps the change record.

4. Change types

  • Standard change: low-risk, routine and repeatable, with a documented procedure that has been pre-approved. Examples: monthly operating system and third-party patching through the RMM, adding a firewall rule from an approved template, renewing a certificate, adding a new workstation built from the standard image, routine antivirus definition updates. Standard changes need no individual approval but are still logged in a ticket.
  • Normal change: any change that is not standard or emergency. Examples: server upgrades, new firewall or VPN configuration, conditional access changes, email migration, line-of-business application upgrades, new Wi-Fi network, changes to backup jobs. Requires risk assessment and approval before implementation.
  • Emergency change: a change that must happen immediately to restore service or stop an active security threat (for example blocking an attacker, applying an actively exploited vulnerability fix, restoring a failed server). May be implemented with verbal approval and documented afterwards.

{{company_name}} maintains the list of pre-approved standard changes, reviewed at least annually by the security lead. A standard change that causes an incident is moved back to the normal category until its procedure is fixed.

5. Change process for normal changes

  1. Request: a change ticket is opened in {{company_name}}'s ticketing system ([system name]) describing what will change, why, systems and users affected, and requested timing.
  2. Risk assessment: the implementer rates the change:
    • Low: affects a single system or few users, easily reversed, no security impact.
    • Medium: affects a department or important system, reversible within the maintenance window, or changes a security setting in a limited way.
    • High: affects the whole company, critical systems (ERP, EHR, email, firewall, identity), Restricted data, or security controls such as MFA, encryption, logging or backups; or is difficult to reverse.
    The assessment includes the security impact of the change (for example whether it opens ports, changes access or disables a control).
  3. Plan: the ticket documents implementation steps, the planned date and time, expected downtime, user communication, testing steps and a backout plan describing how to return to the prior state and how long it takes. For medium and high changes, a current backup or snapshot must be confirmed before starting.
  4. Approval: low changes are approved by the {{company_name}} service lead; medium changes also by the {{client_name}} system owner; high changes also by the security lead or executive sponsor. Approval is recorded in the ticket (email approval replies are attached).
  5. Testing: where practical, the change is tested in a test environment, on a pilot device or group, or by reviewing the vendor's release notes and known issues. High changes should have a documented test result before production.
  6. Communication: users affected by downtime are notified at least [2 business days] in advance for planned changes, including what to expect and who to contact.
  7. Implementation: performed during the approved window (normally [after hours / weekends]). If the change fails or causes problems beyond agreed limits, the backout plan is executed.
  8. Verification: the implementer confirms the change works and that key business functions are operating, and the system owner confirms where appropriate.
  9. Documentation: the ticket is updated with what was actually done, and network diagrams, configuration records, the asset inventory and IT documentation (for example in {{company_name}}'s documentation platform) are updated within 5 business days.
  10. Close: the ticket is closed with the outcome: successful, successful with issues, backed out, or failed.

6. Emergency changes

  • Emergency changes may be approved verbally by the security lead, the executive sponsor, or {{company_name}}'s on-call lead when the client cannot be reached and delay would cause harm.
  • Actions taken during a security incident under the Incident Response Plan are treated as emergency changes.
  • A ticket documenting what was changed, why and who approved it is completed within 1 business day.
  • All emergency changes are reviewed by the security lead and {{company_name}} within 5 business days to confirm the change should remain, and to decide whether follow-up work is needed.

7. Post-implementation review

  • Any change that failed, was backed out, or caused an unplanned outage or security issue receives a post-implementation review within 10 business days, covering what happened, root cause and how the process or procedure will be improved.
  • {{company_name}} reports a summary of changes (count by type, failed changes, emergency changes) at quarterly business reviews.

8. Security configuration baselines

{{company_name}} maintains documented secure configuration baselines for workstations, servers, network devices and the cloud tenant. Changes that deviate from a baseline require normal change approval and are recorded as exceptions. Configuration drift detected by monitoring tools is investigated and corrected or approved.

9. Restrictions on who may make changes

  • Only authorized {{company_name}} staff, approved vendors and designated {{client_name}} administrators may make changes to in-scope systems.
  • Vendors making changes to line-of-business applications must coordinate through {{company_name}} and open or reference a change ticket.
  • Unauthorized changes discovered through monitoring are treated as potential security incidents.

10. Exceptions

Exceptions to this process require approval from the security lead and are documented in the change ticket.

11. Enforcement

Making unauthorized changes to production systems, or bypassing the change process, may result in removal of administrative access and disciplinary action. Vendor violations may be addressed under their contract.
