1. Purpose

The HIPAA Security Rule requires covered entities and business associates to "conduct an accurate and thorough assessment of the potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of electronic protected health information" (45 CFR 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(A)). This document records {{client_name}}'s risk analysis and is the basis for the risk management plan required by 164.308(a)(1)(ii)(B).

Tool option: small practices may use the free HHS Security Risk Assessment (SRA) Tool from ONC/OCR (healthit.gov) instead of, or alongside, this document. If the SRA Tool is used, attach its report and use sections 9–12 below for the risk management plan and approval.

3. Methodology

  1. Identify where ePHI is stored, received, maintained or transmitted (data collection).
  2. Identify and document reasonably anticipated threats and vulnerabilities.
  3. Assess current security measures.
  4. Determine the likelihood of each threat exploiting a vulnerability.
  5. Determine the potential impact.
  6. Determine the level of risk from likelihood and impact.
  7. Document findings in the risk register and plan remediation.

5. Threats and vulnerabilities

Consider threats that are reasonably anticipated for this organization and the vulnerabilities they could exploit. Examples to evaluate:

  • Human, intentional: ransomware and other malware; phishing and business email compromise; credential theft; insider snooping in patient records; theft of devices; hacking of internet-facing systems.
  • Human, unintentional: misdirected email or fax; lost laptop or phone; accidental deletion; misconfigured sharing links; improper disposal.
  • Environmental and natural: fire, flood, earthquake, wildfire and smoke, power outage, HVAC failure (consider local conditions, e.g. PSPS power shutoffs).
  • Technical: hardware failure, unsupported software, internet outage, vendor outage or breach, failed backups.
  • Common vulnerabilities: no MFA on email or remote access; unencrypted laptops or backups; shared logins; unpatched systems; end-of-life operating systems; flat network with medical devices; no audit log review; missing BAAs; untested backups; excessive user permissions.

7. Likelihood and impact scales

7.1 Likelihood (that the threat exploits the vulnerability within the next 12 months)

  • Low: unlikely; strong controls exist or the threat has little motivation or capability. Not expected to occur.
  • Medium: possible; controls exist but have gaps, or the threat is common in the industry. Could occur.
  • High: likely; controls are missing or ineffective and the threat is common and active. Expected to occur.

7.2 Impact (if it occurs)

  • Low: limited effect; few or no records exposed; brief disruption; minimal cost; unlikely to be a reportable breach.
  • Medium: noticeable harm; exposure of a limited number of records; disruption of operations for hours to a day; possible breach notification; moderate cost.
  • High: severe harm; exposure of many records or sensitive data; multi-day outage affecting patient care; breach notification to HHS and media; significant financial, legal or reputational damage.

8. Risk level

Risk level is determined by combining likelihood and impact:

  • High risk: High likelihood with High or Medium impact, or Medium likelihood with High impact.
  • Medium risk: High likelihood with Low impact, Medium likelihood with Medium impact, or Low likelihood with High impact.
  • Low risk: Medium likelihood with Low impact, or Low likelihood with Medium or Low impact.

Target response times: High risks — remediation plan approved within [30] days; Medium — within [90] days; Low — address or formally accept within [12] months.

11. Documentation and retention

This risk analysis, the risk register, the risk management plan and related decisions are retained for at least six years from the date created or the date last in effect, whichever is later (45 CFR 164.316(b)(2)), and made available to those responsible for implementing the procedures.

12. Review cadence

The risk analysis is an ongoing process. It is reviewed and updated at least annually, and whenever there is a significant change — new EHR or major system, new location, office move, merger, new business associate handling ePHI, a security incident, or new regulatory requirements.
